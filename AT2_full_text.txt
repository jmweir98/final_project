1. Problem definition 
 
1.1. Aim 
The aim of this project is to design, implement, and critically evaluate a web-based accessible 
mobility planning platform that integrates explainable route accessibility modelling with venue-level 
accessibility intelligence. The system will combine open geospatial data with structured user-
contributed feedback to support transparent, evidence-informed mobility decision-making for 
individuals with mobility constraints, while explicitly acknowledging data uncertainty and ethical 
considerations. 
 
1.2. Problem description 
Mainstream navigation systems typically optimise journeys based on shortest distance or fastest 
travel time. While effective for general pedestrian use, these tactics do not adequately account for 
accessibility restrictions experienced by wheelchair users and individuals with reduced mobility. 
Factors such as steep gradients, cumulative elevation gain, kerb heights, steps, crossing design, 
pavement width, and surface quality are often ignored or inconsistently represented. For mobility-
constrained users, these factors determine whether a route is feasible rather than simply efficient, 
and routes identified as optimal may in practice be inaccessible or unsafe (Tannert, Kirkham and 
Schöning, 2019). 
Accessibility information relating to destinations is similarly fragmented and unreliable. Although 
platforms such as OpenStreetMap provide structured tags describing features like surface type or 
step-free access, these attributes are inconsistently applied and lack standardisation across 
regions (Goodchild, 2007). Many locations lack sufficient metadata entirely, and raw geospatial 
tags do not essentially translate into meaningful mobility insights. 
Existing community-based platforms provide some insights through user reviews however, these 
are typically subjective, unstructured, and disconnected from route planning functionality. Users 
must therefore consult multiple systems to evaluate route feasibility and destination accessibility, 
increasing effort and uncertainty. This limits the reliability of mobility planning and may discourage 
independent travel (Hara et al., 2016). 
The core problem addressed by this project is that existing data sources contain partial 
accessibility information that is not systematically integrated or analysed within a unified, 
explainable decision-support system. There is a lack of tools that combine quantitative route 
analysis with qualitative accessibility intelligence in a transparent and user-centred manner. 
1.3. Scope 
 
The scope of this project includes the development of a web-based platform capable of generating 
multiple candidate pedestrian routes between a selected origin and destination. The system will 
incorporate elevation sampling and gradient computation using the Open-Meteo Elevation API and 
will retrieve route geometries via OpenRouteService. Derived accessibility metrics will be 
evaluated using a rule-based scoring model to determine route suitability. The platform will 
integrate venue-level accessibility attributes from OpenStreetMap and support a structured user 
feedback mechanism for submitting accessibility observations and optional photographic evidence.

1.4. Intended outcome 
The intended outcome is a web-based application through which users can generate and compare 
pedestrian routes using accessibility-focused metrics, including elevation profiles, gradient 
thresholds, and identified barriers. The system will present clear, interpretable explanations of 
route scores to support informed decision-making. The platform will additionally provide integrated 
venue-level accessibility information and a structured user review workflow enabling community-
contributed insights to enhance local data relevance. 
The system will be evaluated through functional testing, performance assessment, and usability 
review to determine the extent to which it meets its stated objectives. The completed system will 
demonstrate how open geospatial data and community-contributed insights can be combined to 
reduce information fragmentation, improve transparency, and support more confident mobility 
planning.

2. Contextual research 
 
2.1. Context investigation  
 
For individuals with mobility constraints, travel planning involves fundamentally different decision-
making processes compared to general pedestrian navigation. Rather than optimising for speed, 
users must assess whether a route is physically feasible. Environmental factors such as gradient, 
cumulative elevation gain, surface quality, pavement width, and the presence of steps or barriers 
directly influence route viability (Tannert, Kirkham and Schöning, 2019). These considerations 
introduce a form of anticipatory risk assessment, where uncertainty regarding environmental 
conditions can significantly affect user confidence. 
 
Current digital navigation systems do not adequately support this form of decision-making. 
Accessibility features are typically implemented as simplified binary indicators which fail to capture 
the context-dependent nature of accessibility (Demir et al., 2026), increasing cognitive load and 
reducing trust in system outputs. 
 
The increasing availability of open geospatial data presents significant opportunities for addressing 
these limitations. Platforms such as OpenStreetMap provide a rich, collaboratively generated 
dataset containing surface types, pathway classifications, and accessibility-related attributes. 
However, the volunteer-driven nature of these datasets introduces variability in quality, 
completeness, and consistency, with accessibility-related tags frequently missing or outdated 
(Goodchild, 2007). 
 
This presents a substantial barrier to effective mobility planning, potentially discouraging 
independent travel and reducing participation in social activities. In the United Kingdom and 
Northern Ireland, disability equality is supported by the Equality Act 2010 and the Disability 
Discrimination Act 1995. Data handling must comply with GDPR, while ethical considerations 
around transparency, data reliability, and user safety are central to responsible system design. 
 
Within this context, there is a clear and unresolved need for systems that integrate diverse data 
sources into a coherent, transparent, and user-centred decision-support framework, which this

2.2. Supporting theory research 
 
The design of an accessibility-aware mobility planning system is informed by established principles 
from geospatial information systems, route optimisation, and explainable decision-support 
systems. These theoretical foundations provide the technical basis for implementation and the 
conceptual framework for addressing user needs. 
 
Geospatial Information Systems underpin the analysis of spatial data within routing applications. 
Road and pathway networks are typically modelled as graphs composed of nodes and edges, with 
pathfinding algorithms such as Dijkstra's algorithm and A* search used to determine optimal routes 
based on defined cost functions. Accessibility-aware routing requires the extension of these 
models to incorporate additional environmental variables such as gradient, surface type, and the 
presence of physical barriers (Tannert, Kirkham and Schöning, 2019). This introduces a multi-
criteria optimisation problem, where route suitability must be evaluated across multiple competing 
factors, highlighting the importance of transparent and interpretable decision models. 
 
Human-centred design emphasises the importance of aligning system functionality with the 
capabilities, expectations, and decision-making processes of users (Norman, 2013). In 
accessibility-focused systems, this extends beyond interface usability to include the interpretability 
of information and the reduction of cognitive burden. Mobility-constrained users must often make 
decisions under uncertainty, meaning that systems must present information in a way that is 
understandable, supporting the use of clear visualisations and structured explanations within the 
proposed platform. 
 
Explainable decision-support systems are particularly relevant in this context. Research in 
algorithmic transparency demonstrates that users are more likely to trust systems when the 
reasoning behind decisions is visible and interpretable (Doshi-Velez and Kim, 2017). Many existing 
navigation systems operate as black-box models, limiting user understanding of how 
recommendations are generated. In contrast, rule-based scoring approaches enable the explicit 
representation of decision logic, allowing users to understand how factors such as gradient or 
detected barriers influence route suitability. 
 
Volunteer Geographic Information such as OpenStreetMap is characterised by variability in 
accuracy, completeness, and consistency depending on contributor activity (Goodchild, 2007). 
Structured data collection approaches, where users provide information according to predefined 
categories, improve reliability and facilitate integration into decision-support systems, supporting 
the inclusion of a structured feedback mechanism within the proposed solution. 
 
2.3. Similar product review 
 
Several existing systems attempt to address aspects of accessible mobility planning; however, 
they typically focus on either route generation or venue-level accessibility information in isolation. 
Google Maps provides limited accessibility features including wheelchair-accessible routing in 
certain regions. While it benefits from extensive data coverage and a highly intuitive interface, its 
accessibility functionality is fundamentally opaque. Route recommendations are presented without 
explanation of how accessibility factors are evaluated or weighted, preventing users from 
assessing the reliability of suggested routes (Google, 2024).

3.2. Design 
The design of the proposed system adopts a modular, client-server architecture, separating 
presentation, processing, and data management concerns across three layers: a frontend client, a 
backend service layer, and external data services. The frontend is responsible for user interaction, 
map visualisation, and the presentation of route comparisons and accessibility insights. The 
backend manages all application logic, including API integration, accessibility analysis, and route 
scoring. External services provide routing and elevation data via OpenRouteService and the Open-
Meteo Elevation API respectively, with OpenStreetMap providing venue-level geospatial data.  
This architectural approach supports scalability and maintainability while aligning with the system's 
requirement to integrate multiple external data sources and deliver responsive user interactions. 
The system architecture is documented using the C4 model (Brown, 2018), which provides a 
hierarchical approach to describing software systems at increasing levels of detail. A C4 Level 4 
code diagram has not been produced at this stage as the system has not yet been implemented. 
Figure 3.2 illustrates the system context, showing the platform and its relationships with external 
actors and systems. The user interacts with the platform directly, whilst the platform queries 
OpenRouteService for route geometries, the Open-Meteo Elevation API for gradient data, and 
OpenStreetMap for venue-level geospatial information.

Data Design 
A relational data model was selected due to the clearly defined relationships between system 
entities and the requirement to maintain data integrity, consistency, and efficient querying. A 
document store was considered but rejected, as the structured and relational nature of venue, 
review, and image data maps more naturally to a relational schema and supports more reliable 
constraint enforcement. 
Transient data is used during route processing and is not stored permanently. This includes: 
• 
User route inputs (origin and destination) 
• 
Retrieved route geometries 
• 
Elevation samples 
• 
Derived gradient and accessibility metrics 
• 
Computed accessibility scores 
Persistent data is stored to support long-term system functionality and includes: 
• 
Venue: location-based attributes derived from geospatial data 
• 
Review: structured user-contributed accessibility feedback 
• 
Image: optional uploaded images linked to reviews (stored as file references) 
• 
User (optional): if authentication or tracking is implemented 
Core relationships are defined as follows: reviews are associated with specific venues, images are 
linked to reviews, and users are linked to reviews if implemented. These relationships are 
illustrated in Figure 3.5.

A key design decision is to avoid persistent storage of route data and computed metrics, as these 
are dynamically generated per request, ensuring results reflect the most up-to-date external data 
while avoiding unnecessary storage overhead. Images are stored externally with only file 
references retained in the database, reducing the volume of sensitive data held and supporting 
GDPR compliance in alignment with NFR2. 
Accessibility Scoring Model 
The accessibility scoring model applies weighted penalties to gradient thresholds, cumulative 
elevation, and detected barriers to generate a composite route suitability score. A rule-based 
approach was selected over machine learning to ensure transparency and interpretability, enabling 
users to understand and trust scoring decisions (FR5, NFR5). While this reduces adaptability 
compared to machine learning, the lack of high-quality labelled training data and the need for 
explainability make this trade-off acceptable. Machine learning approaches were therefore rejected 
due to data requirements and their inherently opaque outputs, which conflict with system goals 
(Doshi-Velez and Kim, 2017). 
Distance is treated as a secondary factor to prevent excessive detours, ensuring accessibility 
remains the primary optimisation criterion. The model produces both a composite score and a 
contributing factor breakdown directly supporting FR5.

User Experience Design 
The interface centres on an interactive map displaying multiple colour-coded route options, 
aligning with established user expectations from existing navigation systems while minimising the 
learning curve. Colour coding by relative accessibility enables rapid comparison without requiring 
users to interpret numerical data, reducing cognitive load which is a key consideration for users 
making decisions in real-world environments (Norman, 2013). 
Route comparisons are supported by both visual and textual representations including structured 
scoring explanations, ensuring users understand both the outcome and the reasoning behind 
recommendations, directly addressing FR5 and NFR4. Route input is simplified to origin and 
destination selection, minimising user effort, while accessibility feedback is collected through 
structured review forms to ensure data consistency. 
Accessibility considerations embedded throughout include: 
• 
Clear visual hierarchy to prioritise key information 
• 
Readable typography and appropriate contrast levels 
• 
Consistent colour usage to avoid confusion 
• 
Simple interaction flows to reduce cognitive effort

4. Planning 
 
4.1. Methodology 
The development of the mobility planning system follows an Agile methodology (Beck et al., 2001), 
adopting an iterative and incremental approach. A Waterfall methodology was rejected, as 
uncertainty surrounding OpenStreetMap data quality and external API behaviour makes fixed 
upfront specification unreliable. While Agile supports evolving requirements, it introduces risks 
such as scope creep, which are mitigated through iterative refinement (Sommerville, 2016). 
Agile is particularly suitable for this project as it supports continuous refinement of system 
components and enables flexibility when integrating external services such as routing and 
elevation APIs. The potential inconsistency and incompleteness of data introduce uncertainty, 
making a linear methodology less appropriate. In contrast, Agile allows the system to evolve 
through iterative development and ongoing evaluation, with prototyping used to explore key design 
decisions particularly in relation to user interface design and route visualisation. Version control will 
be managed throughout using Git with a remote repository hosted on GitHub, supporting 
incremental development and providing a recoverable history of system changes (Chacon and 
Straub, 2014). 


--- PAGE 22 ---
 
21
The development process is structured into a series of iterations, each focusing on a subset of 
system functionality. Early iterations prioritise core technical components, including route 
generation, elevation analysis, and accessibility scoring, ensuring that high-risk elements are 
addressed at an early stage. Later iterations focus on enhancing usability, integrating user-
generated feedback, and refining system behaviour. Each iteration follows a consistent cycle of 
planning, design, implementation, testing and evaluation, and review and refinement. 
The project is planned across four iterations. Iteration 1 focuses on core routing infrastructure, 
integrating OpenRouteService, retrieving elevation data from Open-Meteo, and implementing the 
rule-based accessibility scoring model. Iteration 2 develops the frontend map interface, route 
comparison view, and score explanation panel. Iteration 3 implements the venue information 
component, user review submission, optional image upload, and authentication. Iteration 4 focuses 
on system-wide testing, usability evaluation, performance assessment, and refinement based on 
evaluation outcomes. Each iteration produces a working increment of the system, with 
requirements reviewed and reprioritised at the start of each cycle in response to findings from the 
previous iteration. The iteration cycle applied throughout development is illustrated in Figure 4.1.

4.2. Initial plans 
The research and requirements phase has involved literature review, analysis of existing systems, 
and definition of system requirements, and is formalised through the submission of this report. 
The design phase will follow, where system architecture, data models, and user interface concepts 
will be developed. This phase must precede implementation to ensure technical decisions are 
grounded in a validated understanding of system requirements and constraints. 
The implementation phase will focus on core functionality, including integration with routing and 
elevation APIs, development of the accessibility scoring model, and validation of route processing.

Once established, development will progress to additional features including user-generated 
reviews, image handling, and authentication. 
Testing and evaluation will be conducted throughout development, with more structured 
assessment in later stages covering functional, performance, and usability testing. The final 
refinement and documentation phase will involve system optimisation, identification of limitations, 
and completion of the project review report. These phases provide the overarching project 
structure, within which four Agile iterations operate during the implementation and testing phases. 
This phased structure will prioritise high-risk core functionality early, ensuring critical components 
such as API integration and scoring logic are validated before additional features are introduced.
